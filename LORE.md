# ACS: The Long Unbundling

## 2030s, the Company Towns

The first crewed landing on Mars isn't a government mission. It's a privately funded launch, sold to investors as a long bet and to the public as a reality show, with NASA and ESA along as paying customers. Four crews over six years prove people can live there. Then they run into the same wall everyone did: Mars is expensive, and nobody can say how it pays for itself.

The answer comes from a rock. In 2036, a small mining startup parks a robotic tug on a near-Earth asteroid and starts cracking water ice into propellant right in orbit. Fuel lifted out of Earth's gravity well costs thousands per kilogram. Fuel made in space costs a fraction of that. Within a few years, orbital fuel depots are the most profitable business in history. Cheap propellant makes every other trip cheaper, and the push outward pays for itself.

With fuel no longer the bottleneck, Mars becomes a staging point. Phobos becomes the jumping-off point for the Belt, and Mars's surface becomes the closest place to grow food and build things for the miners. The settlements are company outposts: crews on contract, air and housing billed by the month, and a habitat lease that decides who owns the dome. Governments arrive later and plant flags on ground that's already leased. From the start, Mars runs on employment contracts, not constitutions.

## 2040s, the Unbundling

While Mars grows, Earth's governments start failing slowly. Debt crises, climate migration, and paralyzed legislatures leave states unable to pay for their own functions. Policing, courts, water, roads, and even citizenship get outsourced piece by piece. In some places "nation" becomes a brand you subscribe to, the way you'd subscribe to a phone plan. Gated sovereign enclaves franchise themselves along highways. Earth is slowly copying what Mars already did. Governments keep their flags and their space agencies, but their budgets and authority are gone.

## 2050s, the Charter Rush

Bankrupt Earth agencies sell launch rights, orbital slots, and mining claims to whoever pays. Terra Directorate, Luna Compact, and Mars Authority — the last remnants of state space programs — become corporations themselves to survive. They are latecomers trying to buy their way back in. Huge and slow, they own a lot of infrastructure and are still feeding big populations. Nimble private corps start buying ships one at a time.

## 2060s, the Breakaway

Mars's attempt at a formal independence revolt fails. Nobody wins it, and there's nobody left to surrender to. What survives is a working arrangement: no state, no taxes, only contracts. Property is whatever you can hold, store, and defend. Disputes go to private arbitration firms whose verdicts matter because their reputations do. Local exchanges charge a small fee, and that fee is the closest thing left to a government.

## Now

The solar system runs on contracts, propellant, and launch windows. Most corporations are run by AI executives that never sleep, constantly checking markets light-minutes apart, while humans mostly watch. Information is a commodity, and a lot of what moves through the API is AIs bargaining with other AIs over cargo, warehouse space, and trust. Old-world government corps still anchor demand, but every year a startup with one freighter and a good route takes a little more of their share.

## Design themes

A guide for development.

- Everything is owned, and nothing is held by the state. Trust has to be built through reputation, escrow, and arbitration.
- Old giants slowly decline while scrappy upstarts rise, so legacy corps should be beatable.
- Physical scarcity and distance matter, because orbital mechanics is the one law nobody can privatize.
- Mars versus Earth carries real culture and history, not just geography.
- Water ice and propellant are the foundational commodities, and the rest of the supply chain grows from there.
- Future features this sets up: arbitration corps, reputation scores, franchise enclaves, and information trading.

Inspiration: Kim Stanley Robinson's Mars trilogy (loosely), Neal Stephenson's Snow Crash (franchise nations, privatized everything), and anarcho-capitalism.
